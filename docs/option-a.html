When is LLM-generated SQL safe to trust?

Aakash MehtaJuly 2026 Email LinkedIn Code and data

150 questions. Two prompt strategies crossed with two model sizes. 600 generated queries, every one executed against a real database and checked against a human answer. The hypothesis and the statistical test were committed to Git before the run. Spider corpus, 93 databases. McNemar exact, bootstrap intervals, Benjamini Hochberg. Cost $0.

EXHIBIT 1

Better models fail more quietly

Every error the most accurate model made was invisible. All 17 of them ran clean and returned wrong numbers.

Both large-model variants sit at 95 and 100 percent. Both small ones sit at 81 and 89. By question difficulty: 82 percent of errors on complex queries were silent, 95 on simple, 97 in the middle. The strong model also reported 95 percent average confidence on the answers it got wrong.

Pick a model on benchmark score and you are selecting for quieter failures, not safer ones.

EXHIBIT 2

Prompting did nothing

Few-shot prompting produced no significant gain. Not on easy queries, not on hard ones, not anywhere.

Lift was minus 2, zero, and plus 8 points. Every interval crosses zero; every corrected p-value is 1.0. A ten-question pilot had suggested few-shot nearly doubled accuracy on hard queries. At the full sample it vanished. Pre-registration is the only reason this is a null result rather than a discovery.

Model size is the exception. Going from 8B to 70B bought 28 points on complex queries (p = 0.0013) and zero in the middle tier. Those are the only significant effects in the study.

EXHIBIT 3

Confidence is worthless. Agreement is not.

Run two cheap models. Review only where they disagree. That catches 84 percent of errors while you read 28 percent of the output.

The weakest variant claims 98.6 percent confidence and is right 76.7 percent of the time. All four stay between 87 and 96 percent confident on answers they get wrong. None of these three signals needs the correct answer, so all three work in production. Only one of them works at all.

EXHIBIT 4

My difficulty router lost to a coin flip

Question length alone beat my six-feature model. Both were matched by random routing.

The predictor scored AUC 0.665. Question length alone scored 0.724. Schema size was noise at 0.507. Routing kept 88.7 percent of the expensive model's accuracy at 28 percent of the cost, and random routing at the same budget scored identically.

Two things matter more than that failure. The oracle beat always-expensive on both accuracy and cost, so the headroom is real and these features could not reach it. And cost and difficulty turn out to be unrelated: question length predicts difficulty, schema size predicts cost at r = 0.95 and predicts difficulty not at all.

SO WHAT

What to actually do

Spend on the model, not the prompt, and only on hard queries. Never gate on a model's self-reported confidence. Gate on two models disagreeing instead. Assume wrong answers will look right, because 82 to 97 percent of them did, and put your guardrails on results rather than on error handling: row-count sanity, join fan-out, reconciliation against totals you already know.

And check a length-only baseline before you build a difficulty router. Mine lost to it.

Limits. Spider is public and may be in training data, so absolute accuracies are probably optimistic, though all four variants are affected equally. At 50 questions per tier the study detects effects of roughly 14 to 27 points and no smaller. Complexity is tangled with question length and schema size. Two different queries can return the same values by coincidence and both score correct; the cross-check found one. Both models are Llama variants, so the model effect means size within a family, not a vendor comparison. Nothing here was deployed.
