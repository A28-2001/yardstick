The best model got it wrong silently.

150 questions. Two prompt strategies, two model sizes, 600 generated SQL queries, every one run against a real database. Prompting changed nothing. The most accurate model made the fewest mistakes and not one of them was visible.

Valid SQL. Right table. Real job titles. It answers a different question, and the model reported maximum confidence. A query that crashes gets investigated. This one goes into a dashboard.

02

Better models fail more quietly

Every error the most accurate model made was invisible. All 17.

FIG 1   Share of each variant's errors that executed cleanly and returned the wrong rows, ordered by accuracy. n = 150 per variant.

Both large-model variants sit at 95 and 100 percent. Both small ones sit at 81 and 89. By question difficulty: 82 percent of errors on complex queries were silent, 95 percent on simple, 97 percent in the middle tier.

The strong model also reported 95 percent average confidence on the answers it got wrong. Choose a model on benchmark score and you are selecting for quieter failures, not safer ones.

03

Prompting did nothing. Size did.

Few-shot prompting produced no significant gain at any difficulty.

Lift was minus 2, zero, and plus 8 points across the three tiers. Every confidence interval crosses zero and every corrected p-value is 1.0. A ten-question pilot had suggested few-shot nearly doubled accuracy on hard queries; at the full sample it vanished. The hypothesis was committed to Git before the run, which is the only reason this appears as a null result rather than a discovery.

FIG 2   Percentage-point differences with 95 percent bootstrap intervals. Upper block is the pre-registered comparison, corrected. Lower block is the exploratory model effect.

Model size is the exception, and only on hard queries. Going from 8B to 70B bought 28 points on complex queries (95% CI [+14, +42], p = 0.0013) and exactly zero in the middle tier, where all four variants land between 0.82 and 0.84. Those are the only significant effects in the entire study.

04

Never trust a model's own confidence

Ask two models instead. Review only where they disagree.

The weakest variant claims 98.6 percent confidence and is right 76.7 percent of the time. All four stay between 87 and 96 percent confident on the answers they get wrong. Confidence carries almost no information about whether a specific query is right.

None of these three needs the correct answer, so all three work in production. Only one of them works at all. Run two cheap models, compare what their queries actually return, and send the disagreements to review: 28 percent reviewed, 84 percent of errors caught, 16 percent false alarms.

05

My router lost to a coin flip

Question length alone beat my six-feature difficulty model.

The predictor scored AUC 0.665. A single feature, question length, scored 0.724. Schema size was noise at 0.507. Routing kept 88.7 percent of the expensive model's accuracy at 28 percent of its cost, and random routing at the same budget scored identically. Reported as the negative result it is.

FIG 4   Six routing policies on the held-out split. Difficulty routing is cheaper than random and no more accurate. The oracle is both cheaper and more accurate than always using the expensive model.

Two things here matter more than the failure. The oracle beat always-expensive on both axes, so the headroom is real and these features simply could not reach it. And cost and difficulty turn out to be unrelated: question length predicts difficulty, schema size predicts cost at r = 0.95 and predicts difficulty not at all. Input tokens are 95 percent of spend because the schema goes into every prompt.

06

What to actually do

Spend on the model, not the prompt, and only on hard queries. Never gate on self-reported confidence. Gate on two models disagreeing instead. Assume wrong answers will look right, because 82 to 97 percent of them did, and put your guardrails on results rather than on error handling: row-count sanity, join fan-out checks, reconciliation against totals you already know. Check a length-only baseline before building a difficulty router, because mine lost to one.

Limits. Spider is public and may sit in training data, so absolute accuracies are probably optimistic, though all four variants are affected equally. At 50 questions per tier this detects effects of roughly 14 to 27 points and no smaller. Complexity is tangled with question length and schema size. Two different queries can return identical values by coincidence and both score correct; the cross-check found one. Both models are Llama variants, so the model effect means size within a family rather than a vendor comparison. Nothing here was deployed.
